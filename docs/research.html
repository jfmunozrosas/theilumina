Research problems and questions

Why reliable estimation and inference for Theil indices is still an open problem

Modified

November 16, 2026

Theil indices (T_0 and T_1) are the standard tool to decompose inequality into a between-group and a within-group component. Yet the way they are usually estimated and used in practice raises several problems that the project addresses. The figures on this page are preliminary results of the team, and illustrate the problems with the most skewed distribution considered (Pareto).

How to read the figures

The relative bias (RB) of an estimator is the percentage difference between its average value over many simulated samples and the true value of the index. Dashed horizontal lines delimit the region of negligible bias. Each panel corresponds to a level of inequality (T_0 or T_1 from 0.05 to 0.5), and the horizontal axis shows the sample size.

Problem 1 · The estimators underestimate inequality

The standard (plug-in) estimators of the Theil indices are biased downwards in finite samples: they systematically underestimate inequality. The bias grows with the skewness of the data and the level of inequality, which are precisely the conditions found in income, wealth, water consumption or tourism data. With highly skewed data and high inequality, samples larger than 5,000 observations are needed for the bias to become negligible.

Research questions (WP1)

  • RQ1. How large is the bias of the estimators of T_0 and T_1, and which data features (sample size, skewness, inequality, tails) drive it?
  • RQ2. Can the bias be predicted from observable sample features, so that users know in advance whether their estimate is reliable (negligible, mild, moderate or severe bias)?
  • RQ3. Which correction (jackknife, model-based, analytical or semiparametric) best reduces the bias without inflating the mean squared error?
  • RQ4. How does the bias propagate to the between- and within-group components of the decomposition?

Problem 2 · Inference is unreliable: variances are underestimated

Even when the point estimate is acceptable, its margin of error may not be. Under skewed distributions, the usual asymptotic and bootstrap variance estimators severely underestimate the true variance (by 30–70% in the figure below), even with thousands of observations. As a result, confidence intervals are too narrow and differences in inequality across regions, groups or years may be declared significant when they are not. The jackknife variance estimator remains close to the region of negligible bias.

Research questions (WP2)

  • RQ5. Under which combinations of sample size, skewness and inequality do the asymptotic, bootstrap, semiparametric and jackknife variance estimators become unreliable?
  • RQ6. Can new jackknife-based variance estimators and confidence intervals achieve coverage close to the nominal level under strong skewness and inequality, both for the indices and for their decomposition?

Problem 3 · The jackknife is computationally prohibitive

The jackknife recomputes the estimator n times, leaving out one observation each time, so its cost grows quadratically with the sample size (\mathcal{O}(n^2)). With n = 100{,}000 observations, the standard jackknife needs more than 16 minutes to compute the variance of a single Theil index, and a Monte Carlo study with n = 5{,}000 takes more than two days. Our closed-form expressions give exactly the same result with linear cost (\mathcal{O}(n)): 15 milliseconds and less than 40 seconds, respectively. For the bias-corrected estimators, the standard jackknife would take decades, which makes reduced expressions essential.

Research questions (WP3)

  • RQ7. How large is the computational cost of the standard jackknife compared with alternative variance estimators for big data (surveys and administrative records with millions of observations)?
  • RQ8. Can exact, closed-form jackknife expressions be derived for the bias-corrected estimators, the decomposition components and complex survey designs, with no loss of accuracy?

Problem 4 · There is no open software for reliable Theil inference

Existing software computes point estimates of Theil indices but does not offer bias diagnostics, bias corrections or reliable variance estimators and confidence intervals, and it does not scale to large samples.

Research question (WP4)

  • RQ9. Can the methods developed in WP1–WP3 be implemented in an open, documented and efficient R package (theil, with a C++ core) that makes reliable inference for Theil indices accessible to applied researchers?

Problem 5 · Do corrected estimates change empirical conclusions?

The bias is not a technicality. When the methods are applied to real data, the difference between standard and bias-corrected estimates varies with sample size, skewness and inequality, and can change rankings and conclusions. For example, in our application to real GDP per capita for 183 countries (1990–2023), the bias-corrected estimates are between 2% and 6% higher for T_0, and up to 12% higher for T_1, than the standard ones, with the largest differences in the most skewed regions (the Americas and Asia & Pacific).

Research questions (WP5)

  • RQ10. How unequal are household water consumption and expenditure between and within municipalities, and how much does the bias correction change this picture?
  • RQ11. Does tourism reduce or increase income inequality within and between destinations?
  • RQ12. How unequal are servitization, digital transformation and AI adoption across firms, sectors and countries?
  • RQ13. Is there global convergence in per capita consumption and income once bias-corrected estimators and reliable confidence intervals are used?

See Objectives, work packages and activities for how these questions are organised into work packages.
