Current status: under evaluation. The research project proposal was submitted to the 2026 call for Knowledge Generation Projects (Spanish State Plan for Scientific, Technical and Innovation Research). Last update: 16 November 2026.

The research problems and questions that motivate these objectives are described in Research problems and questions.

The project is organised into five work packages (WP), each aligned with a General Objective (GO). Each WP sets out Specific Objectives (SO) and Activities (A). A cross-cutting activity covers this website:

  • A1. Update the project’s interactive website to disseminate results, share data and code, and support reproducibility.

WP1 · Bias diagnosis and correction

GO1. Identify, predict and correct the bias of Theil index estimators

Researchers: E. Álvarez, R. Cano, R.M. García, P.J. Moya, A.J. López, J.F. Muñoz

Hypotheses. H1. Skewness and inequality of the data affect the bias of the Theil indices, and the impact is greater as both increase jointly. H2. The bias of \hat{T}_1 is more sensitive than that of \hat{T}_0. H3. The bias of the Theil indices can be predicted from sample characteristics using GAM models. H4. The jackknife method effectively reduces the bias problem in the estimation of the Theil indices. H5. The mean squared error of the corrected estimators does not increase substantially with respect to the standard estimators. H6. The bias is transmitted unevenly to the between-group and within-group components.

  • SO1. Quantify the bias of the estimators and the data features that cause it, comparing the sensitivity of T_0 and T_1.
    • A2. Design the Monte Carlo experiment.
    • A3. Relative bias (RB) and bias ratio (BR) of T_0 and T_1 (H1, H2).
  • SO2. Predict the bias from observable sample features, as a diagnostic tool.
    • A4. GAM models for the bias.
    • A5. Qualitative bias scale: negligible, mild, moderate, severe (H3).
  • SO3. New bias-correction techniques and their impact on the MSE.
    • A6. Jackknife and GAM-based corrected estimators.
    • A7. Comparison with analytical and semiparametric corrections (H4, H5).
  • SO4. Bias in the between- and within-group decomposition.
    • A8. Bias of the decomposition components (H6).

WP2 · Reliable inference

GO2. Reliable inference for Theil indices under strong skewness and inequality

Researchers: E. Álvarez, J. Chica, R.M. García, I. García, P.J. Moya, J.F. Muñoz, F. Vendrell, V.E. Yáñez

Hypotheses. H7. The bias of the variance estimators of the Theil indices and the under-coverage of confidence intervals increase with skewness and inequality, and persist even with large samples. H8. The jackknife and the bias-corrected estimators make it possible to construct confidence intervals with coverage close to the nominal level under strong skewness, without substantially increasing their width.

  • SO5. Bias of variance estimators and coverage of confidence intervals.
    • A9. Evaluation of asymptotic, bootstrap, semiparametric and jackknife variances.
    • A10. Scenarios where inference becomes unreliable (H7).
  • SO6. New variance estimators and confidence intervals.
    • A11. Jackknife variances and new confidence intervals.
    • A12. Comparison and recommendations (H8).

WP3 · Computationally efficient inference

GO3. Reduce the computational cost of the jackknife

Researchers: E. Álvarez, P.A. Cano, J. Chica, I. García, A.J. López, E. Maroto, P.J. Moya, J.F. Muñoz

Hypotheses. H9. The \mathcal{O}(n^2) cost of the jackknife limits its application in large samples and in simulations. H10. It is possible to obtain jackknife expressions for the Theil indices that avoid recomputing them n times and reduce their cost to \mathcal{O}(n), without loss of accuracy.

  • SO7. Computational cost of the standard jackknife compared with alternative methods.
    • A13. Execution times (H9).
  • SO8. Efficient jackknife expressions with no loss of accuracy.
    • A14. Analytical jackknife expressions.
    • A15. Implementation and validation (H10).

WP4 · The R package theil

GO4. Open, efficient and reproducible software

Researchers: E. Álvarez, J. Chica, I. García, A.J. López, P.J. Moya, J.A. Salinas, J.F. Muñoz

Hypotheses. H11. It is possible to develop open software, with a C++ core and low computational cost, that integrates all the contributions of the literature and of this project into a single tool. H12. This reproducible software will make it easier for researchers, statistical institutes and public bodies to adopt the new methods. It will also provide more reliable results when the bias is appreciable and will make inference feasible in large samples.

  • SO9. R package theil, with a C++ core (Rcpp).
    • A16. Development of the package (H11, H12).
    • A17. Documentation with vignettes and release on CRAN (H12).

WP5 · Multidisciplinary transfer

GO5. Transfer to the Social Sciences

Researchers by transfer area:
Water: J. Guardiola, N. Ibáñez, S. López, J.A. Rodríguez, C. Tortajada
Tourism: R.M. García, C.M. Llorca, A.C. Casas, T. Addabbo, J.A. Rodríguez, J.A. Salinas, J. Chica, R. Cano
Firms: F. Vendrell, E. Gomes, J.F. Muñoz, E. Álvarez

The methods developed in WP1–WP4 are applied to real data in several areas of the Social Sciences. The specific objectives SO10–SO12 are a sample of the transfer objectives of the project, chosen according to the expertise of the researchers in each area.

Hypotheses. H13. Tariff heterogeneity across municipalities explains part of the inequality in water expenditure, and inequality in consumption is concentrated within municipalities. H14. Tourism reduces income inequality within destinations and increases it between destinations, and both effects are statistically distinguishable. H15. Servitization, digitalization and AI widen the performance gap between the firms that adopt them and those that do not, and increase heterogeneity among adopters. H16. Because of the bias in skewed distributions, global convergence in per capita consumption is lower than uncorrected estimates indicate.

  • SO10. Water: inequality in household water consumption and expenditure between and within municipalities.
    • A18. Estimation of water consumption and expenditure inequality and its between- and within-municipality decomposition, using bias-corrected estimators and confidence intervals (H13).
  • SO11. Tourism: effect of tourism on income inequality within and between destinations.
    • A19. Measurement and decomposition of income inequality in tourist destinations, and analysis of the effect of tourism on its evolution (H14).
  • SO12. Firms: servitization, digital transformation and AI adoption.
    • A20. Measurement and decomposition of inequality in the adoption of servitization, digital technologies and AI across firms, sectors and countries (H15).
  • SO13. Global convergence in per capita consumption with bias-corrected estimators.
    • A21. Analysis of global convergence in per capita consumption and income with bias-corrected Theil indices and confidence intervals (H16).
